1. How can computational methods help organize, represent, and retrieve non-web-native materials?
By non-web-native materials, I refer to three overlapping categories at different levels:
- File formats: Materials not originally structured in web markup languages (e.g., HTML), such as PDFs, images, and other non-HTML files.
- Forms of representation: Multi-format or multimodal digital artifacts, such as library and museum objects represented through combinations of images, text, metadata, and other contextual information.
- Origins of materials: Non-born-digital materials that have been digitized, often with varying scan quality, page layouts, and degrees of machine readability.
    <br>

    <br>
2. How should we govern a “data commons”? Why, how, and if we should scrape (or archive) ALL THE INTERNET?[^note] And if we could establish some form of data commons, what kinds of search and recommendation systems should we build on top of it?
- <mark>Amendment: Which layers of our digital information systems should serve as shared public infrastructure? How might a communally governed data foundation support an open ecosystem of search, recommendation, LLM applications (or rejections), and interfaces? e.g. What can projects like OpenStreetMap, Common Crawl, or even the AT Protocol teach us about how to build better systems?</mark>
    <br>

    <br>
3. What is the history of the Cold War information technology race, and what that might have to do with the FOSS (Free and Open-source Software) movement?[^note1]
- <mark>Amendment: To take a step back and examine the political economy and history of software, approaching FOSS through both the canonical Stallman–Raymond debate and FOSS as an ethos and a practice within particular fields, for example, Zotero and Omeka from the Roy Rosenzweig Center of George Mason University.</mark>[^note2]
