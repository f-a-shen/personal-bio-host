“What’s your method?”

I’ve always found this an interesting question to ask and to be asked, as somebody whose research combines humanistic inquiry and engineering solutions in proportions I’m still trying to understand. It has become a less abstract question now that I have graduate school applications to finish.

I’ve attempted to frame my research passions within disciplinary boxes, only to find my list of candidates widening: history of ideas, intellectual history, science and technology studies, computational culture and heritage, human-centered design and engineering, computer-supported cooperative work, library and information science, archival studies, digital humanities, textual and digital studies, and human-computer interaction—you name one. The list keeps growing.

I know that a discipline is not a method. But when I try to explain how I work, the question keeps becoming one of where I belong. The more I explore these overlapping, sometimes seemingly orthogonal categories, the harder it becomes to fit my passions and skills neatly within them.

> All research is me-search

Perhaps, if I were given the opportunity to establish an academic discipline, I would appoint myself chair of the Department of “Why Do Other Departments Exist?”

This certain meta-ness follows me through my research. I started out wanting to be a historian, only to write a thesis about how historical knowledge, and the intellectuals who produce it—are shaped by political power and some of their works did not make any sense except, let's say, justifying the Vietnam  War. [^1]

Trying to become a historian, I veered into the archives. I became interested in what happens when we (historians) treat archives as institutions to be studied, rather than simply as authoritative starting points for studying something else. Then, trying to become an archivist, I found myself drawn to building archives to which I felt a personal sense of belonging and connection.[^2]

The urge to share, not just preserve, brought me to digital methods and software building. There, I encountered another tension: how to work at the scale of tens of millions of documents while preserving what makes each one particular: its font, layout, relationships and textuality.

Even in my own notes, I keep building systems of organization and then getting lost in them. My never-ending pursuit of the perfect personal knowledge management system took me from OneNote to Notion to Obsidian, then to attempting to build my own software, complete with metadata tagging and a massive relational database. At some point, I gave up and decided to write all my notes in one long Markdown file, which I jokingly named “The Scroll.”

After all, with all the exuberance and exhaustion they brought, I start to wonder: am I departing away from the same question or somehow on a path returning to it?

<div class="story-break" aria-hidden="true"></div>

Then there is a deadline, a bunch of boxes to click, a few names to mention, and a couple of hundred words to put down. How exactly do I frame myself in a way that is productive and clear, both to me as the applicant and to the people reviewing my application?

“What’s my methodology?” I’ve developed an ad hoc way of sorting the possibilities: reading and theorizing; qualitative research, including interviews and field studies; sociobehavioral experiments; and research through designing and building engineering solutions. These categories like any category are flawed. Still, they give me somewhere to begin.

Sure. Now pick one and fit myself in. Tell that part of my story, however flawed the categorization might be. "C’mon, get through December; you’ll be good," the little man in me keeps telling me.

Settling on one or a few methods is only the beginning. Am I looking for a person, a lab, or a program? What kind of relationship does each offer?

Then comes the question of how to introduce myself. Should I emphasize my character and intellectual curiosity, beginning with an anecdote as I might in an undergraduate personal statement? Or should I write a detailed research proposal, demonstrating what I could contribute to a particular PI’s work and a lab’s ongoing projects? It’s hard to tell the means apart from the ends.

> “Did you love me as the artist or as the man?”  
> 
 >—David Shayne [in a desperate attempt to win back his girlfriend, Ellen], _Bullets over Broadway_ (1994)

To temporarily put my perplexity on hold, I keep returning to two conversations.

During a period when I was certain I wanted to become a history PhD, I proposed a thought experiment to Prof. L, who came to UW with a specialization in Digital History. 

I had been thinking about the evolution of digital methods and the labor involved in historical research: from traveling to archives, moving boxes, flipping through papers, to operating microfilm machines, from reading digitized documents, then to keyword searching text made accessible through OCR.

"What if information technology kept reducing human labor by orders of magnitude? What if work that once took months could eventually be done in seconds by the machine itself? Therefore, would there still be a need for historians? What would that mean for graduate education?"

As I remember it, Prof. L thought for a moment and said, “I believe the product of a history PhD is the historian themself.”

And during another period when I was certain I did not want to become a history PhD, I asked Prof. HF, somewhat worried about staying connected (or with some audacity, staying relevant to the field): "What I would do if I lost my library privileges. Would I still be invited to conferences?"

It was during the upheaval over higher education funding in 2025. His answer, as I remember it, was: “Maybe all of those things--the conferences, the papers--will go away. But I believe what makes a good historian is their ability to keep reading and thinking.”
